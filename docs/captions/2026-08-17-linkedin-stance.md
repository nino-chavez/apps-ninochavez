"They stop where judgment starts."

That sentence is doing the most work on a page I put up recently: apps.ninochavez.co, home for two small tools I built for my own work and then made available — Cutting Board, a prep station for a day of event footage, and Yawn, a private meeting notepad for the Mac. Both are internal alpha builds. The sentence is the design position.

Neither tool decides anything. One proposes a starting order for footage; every keep, highlight, and reject call stays yours. The other records a consented meeting and keeps the transcript beside your notes — it does not join calls, read a calendar, or turn the conversation into a task list. In both, analysis narrows what deserves your attention, then stops. It does not decide what is good, and it does not act on your behalf.

The other boundary is where the work lives. Everything runs on your machine. Your files stay where they are, nothing uploads on its own, and no account is required.

Both boundaries cost features, and both come from real use rather than a category that looked interesting. Each of these started as a job I do regularly that no tool owned, with workarounds costing hours — a weekend of event video is hours of scrubbing before the first cut gets made, and no tool owned that hour.

Both apps are labeled what they are: internal alphas, one invite-only, one downloadable. The rest of the position is on the page.
